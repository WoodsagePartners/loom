## Opportunity finding as the front end

Every method assumes you have already chosen the territory. Simplexity starts at problem finding. Design thinking starts at empathy. Neither tells you which problem deserves the effort. **Opportunity finding sits upstream of all of them, and it is where organisations actually flounder.**

Critical distinction, from Vogt, Brown and Isaacs: opportunity finding is not problem finding. Isaacs notes there is "a subtle feeling of disempowerment in a problem, a feeling that all the doors are shut." Leaders who ask *what is possible here and who cares* get radically different energy than those asking *what is wrong here and who is responsible*.

For engineering and PMO-governed cultures this matters enormously. They are excellent at root cause analysis, so their reflex is deficit framing. Walk in asking what is broken and you get a defensive risk register.

### Do not generate questions. Harvest them.

A question generator produces generic questions, and generic questions get the answers people already had ready. The test that kills it: *is this a genuine question, one to which we really do not know the answer?*

But any large organisation is already full of questions people are privately chewing on and have never had anywhere to put. That is the raw ore, and it is free.

Opening move: **what are you already wondering that you have never had anywhere to put?**

Machine assistance belongs later, and it is variation not generation. Take a human's question and run it systematically through the three dimensions. Same question at five altitudes. Same question with the assumption inverted. Same question with every preposition swapped. The human picks which version has heat.

## The question ladder

A ladder is a sequence where **each question is only answerable because of the one below it.**

The test: shuffle them. If the order does not matter, you wrote a survey. If shuffling breaks it, you built a ladder.

Three things climb at once. **Power**, from concrete recall up to why. **Scope**, from your own hands out to the enterprise. **Abstraction**, from a remembered Tuesday to a structural assumption to a possibility.

A worked shape, built for a distributed offshore workforce:

1. When was the last time you saw something on the job and thought there has to be a better way? *(when, personal, safe)*
2. What did that cost? Time, money, risk, or aggravation. *(what, concrete)*
3. What is true about how we work that lets that keep happening? *(assumption surfacing)*
4. What would someone who has never worked in this industry find strange about that? *(the outsider move, and your own position weaponised)*
5. Where does something like this already work well here, and what is different about it? *(appreciative flip)*
6. If this were completely solved, what would become possible that is not today? *(possibility)*
7. What question, if we answered it, would make the most difference to the next five years here? *(handoff to clustering)*

Practical open question: release the rungs one at a time or all at once? All at once is easier but flattens back into a survey, because people can see the destination and write toward it. Two or three batches is probably the compromise.

### Six properties of a question that travels

Derived from the HP case, where "best industrial research lab **in** the world" became "best **for** the world" and spread across the whole company.

1. **Scope elasticity.** It works at every altitude. If it only makes sense at exec level, it dies at exec level.
2. **It generates more questions.** Answering it opens territory rather than closing it.
3. **One breath.** If you cannot repeat it from memory in a hallway, it cannot travel. Travel is a verbal phenomenon.
4. **Genuine unknowing.** If leadership already has the answer, everyone can smell it.
5. **It touches values, not metrics.** People have no energy for questions that are only about removing pain.
6. **It reframes rather than accepts.** It challenges an assumption the organisation did not know it held.

These are testable, so they work as a scoring filter on candidate questions.

### Clustering

After harvest, cluster in **silence**. Fifteen minutes, iterative, everyone moving at once. Proven in San Diego, July 2026.

Silence removes the verbal channel where seniority lives. Everyone works in parallel. Disagreement gets expressed by moving something rather than arguing. Nobody owns the taxonomy so everyone owns the result. Naming too early collapses it.

Two consequences worth holding onto. **Never automate this.** A machine that clusters and names steals the moment the whole workshop exists for. And note that silent grouping is non-verbal, parallel and iterative, which makes it the one workshop activity that translates to async almost natively. It would work across three rotations over forty-eight hours.

The output is framed **as questions, never as problems.** That keeps the front end genuinely method agnostic instead of secretly being Simplexity step one wearing a hat.

## Hero's Journey, mapped to an innovation engagement

Client is the hero. Facilitator is the mentor at step four and gone by step twelve.

| # | Stage | In the work |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ordinary World | How it has always been done here |
| 2 | Call to Adventure | The challenge surfaces |
| 3 | Refusal | We tried that. That is regulation. |
| 4 | Meeting the Mentor | Method arrives, scaffold goes up |
| 5 | Crossing the Threshold | Committing to look again |
| 6 | Tests, Allies, Enemies | Fact finding. Which walls are real? |
| 7 | Approach the Cave | Closing on the actual problem |
| 8 | **The Ordeal** | **The original question dies** |
| 9 | The Reward | A question worth answering |
| 10 | The Road Back | Ideas, ranked and owned |
| 11 | Resurrection | Proving it where it counts |
| 12 | Return with the Elixir | They run it without you |

Step eight is the load bearing one. The moment a team's original question dies is what a real reframe actually feels like, and it is the thing worth charging for. Step three, Refusal, is where "we tried that" and "that is a regulation" live, which makes it the natural home for assumption testing and the graveyard walk.

Note the journey is probably not monotonic. Real threads reframe more than once, so steps six through nine likely cycle.

## Zone of Proximal Development and the resident model

The fit is tighter than most consulting frames, because of one thing people forget about Vygotsky: **the scaffold is designed to come down.** Gradual release of responsibility. I do, we do, you do. That is the Resident Model with a citation attached.

It also sharpens the existing contrast. A parachute consultant does the work outside the zone entirely, hands over the answer, and nothing transfers. A resident works inside the zone, which is the only place learning happens.

### The reciprocal zone

On method, the facilitator is the more knowledgeable other. On the client's industry, the facilitator is the novice and they are the experts. This is not a weakness to paper over, it is the pitch:

> "I do not know your business, and that is exactly why this works. I cannot hand you answers. I can only hand you better questions, which means every answer in this room will be yours."

Three things happen at once. It disarms the credibility objection before anyone raises it. It makes them the experts, which is the ownership transfer you needed anyway. And it models deferral of judgment on yourself, day one.

### Warning

ZPD is an **internal design tool**. Never say it to the client. You do not walk into a room of engineers and imply you are the teacher and they are the learners. Internally: scaffolding. Externally: we solve your real problem together and you keep the method.

### Sequence

1. **Diagnose the zone.** You cannot scaffold a zone you have not located. Basadur profiles as the instrument. Also produces a first artifact that tells them something true about themselves in week one.
2. **Model before you assign.** Run the first real problem in front of them, narrating why you are doing each step.
3. **Fade on purpose and name it.** Session one you run it. Session four a team member runs a step. Session eight you are in the corner. Naming the fade at the start makes it read as design rather than disengagement.
4. **Their win, not yours.** Design the first thirty days around something they present upward. Air cover is oxygen.

Measure behaviour change, whether the team can run the method without you, and whether problems solve faster with more alignment. Agree those before the sponsor invents ideas-per-quarter.
